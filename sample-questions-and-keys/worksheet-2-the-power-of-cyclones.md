## The Power of Cyclones

Read the passage carefully, then answer the questions in full sentences.

---

Every year, between November and April, tropical cyclones form over the warm waters north of New Zealand. Most of them stay well away from land, spinning themselves out over the open ocean. But every so often, one tracks far enough south to bring heavy rain, strong winds, and flooding to New Zealand's coasts.

A cyclone begins as a cluster of thunderstorms over warm ocean water. As the warm, moist air rises, it creates an area of low pressure below it. Cooler air rushes in to fill the gap, and because of the way the Earth spins, this incoming air is pushed into a spiral. If the ocean stays warm enough, the storm keeps gathering energy and can grow into a full cyclone, with winds that circle a calm centre known as the eye.

New Zealand is usually too far south and the surrounding water too cool for cyclones to survive at full strength by the time they arrive. Instead, they often weaken into what scientists call an "ex-tropical cyclone" — still capable of dumping enormous amounts of rain in a short time, even without the fierce winds of a full cyclone.

Meteorologists track these storms using satellites, weather balloons, and ocean buoys, watching for changes in wind speed, pressure, and direction. This tracking gives coastal communities days of warning, enough time to prepare sandbags, secure outdoor furniture, and, if necessary, evacuate low-lying areas.

Climate scientists have noted that as ocean temperatures rise, cyclones may be able to hold more moisture and travel further before weakening. This means understanding and preparing for these storms is becoming more important for New Zealand each year, not less.
