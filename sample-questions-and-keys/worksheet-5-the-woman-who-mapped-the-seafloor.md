## The Woman Who Mapped the Seafloor

Read the passage carefully, then answer the questions in full sentences.

---

For most of history, people believed the bottom of the ocean was flat and featureless — a dark, empty plain far below the waves. Marie Tharp, a scientist working in the United States in the 1950s, would prove them completely wrong.

At the time, women were rarely allowed onto research ships, so Tharp worked from an office on land, studying data brought back by her male colleagues. Ships had begun using sonar, bouncing sound waves off the ocean floor to measure its depth. Tharp's job was to take thousands of these depth readings and turn them into a map.

As she plotted the numbers by hand, a startling pattern appeared: a long, deep valley running down the middle of a mountain range stretching across the ocean floor. Tharp believed this valley was a rift — a place where the Earth's crust was slowly pulling apart. At the time, most scientists dismissed this idea as impossible, calling it "girl talk." The theory of continents drifting apart was considered fringe science, not something a serious researcher should support.

Tharp didn't stop working. She kept refining her maps, and further evidence continued to support what she had found. Eventually, her rift valley became one of the key pieces of evidence for plate tectonics — the now widely accepted theory that the Earth's crust is made of moving plates, constantly shifting, colliding, and pulling apart over millions of years.

Tharp's work transformed how scientists understood the entire planet, yet she received little recognition during much of her career. Today, her maps are celebrated as some of the most important scientific achievements of the twentieth century, and she is remembered as one of the pioneers who revealed the hidden shape of the ocean floor.
