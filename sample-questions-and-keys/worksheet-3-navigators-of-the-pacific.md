## Navigators of the Pacific

Read the passage carefully, then answer the questions in full sentences.

---

Long before compasses or satellite maps existed, Polynesian voyagers crossed thousands of kilometres of open ocean to find and settle islands scattered across the Pacific, including Aotearoa New Zealand. They did this without any of the instruments sailors rely on today. Instead, they read the ocean and sky like a map.

Navigators memorised the positions of stars as they rose and set, using them to hold a steady course through the night. During the day, they watched the sun's path, the direction and pattern of ocean swells, and the way waves bounced off distant, unseen islands to change the shape of the sea's surface. Even birds gave clues — certain seabirds fly out to feed each morning and return to land each evening, so their flight paths could point a canoe toward land long before it came into view.

Building the canoes themselves required deep knowledge too. Double-hulled voyaging canoes, sometimes called waka hourua, were designed to be stable in rough seas and capable of carrying enough people, plants, and animals to start a new settlement. Everything needed for survival — kūmara, taro, dogs, and more — had to be carefully packed and cared for on journeys that could last weeks.

This knowledge was not written down. It was passed from master navigators to apprentices through years of practice, often starting in childhood, memorising star paths and practising judgement of the swells until the skills became second nature. A single mistake in reading the ocean could mean missing an island entirely.

Today, groups across the Pacific, including in Aotearoa, have revived these traditional navigation skills. Voyaging canoes such as Hōkūle'a have sailed across the Pacific using only traditional methods, proving that this ancient knowledge still works — and ensuring it is not lost.
